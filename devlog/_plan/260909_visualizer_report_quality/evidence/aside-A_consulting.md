# Professional Consulting Report and Slide Writing Standards: McKinsey, Bain, and BCG

## 1. Findings

### 1.1 Barbara Minto and the Pyramid Principle
The Pyramid Principle was formulated by Barbara Minto during her tenure at McKinsey & Company as the firm's first female post-MBA hire (Harvard Business School class of 1963). It provides the foundational logic for executive communication, consulting presentations, and structured report writing.

* **Primary Concept and Definition**:
  According to Barbara Minto's official publication, "The Minto Pyramid Principle says that your thinking will be easy for a reader to grasp if you present the ideas organized as a pyramid under a single point" (barbaraminto.com, Concept). Thinking naturally progresses bottom-up by grouping similar facts and extracting insights, but effective communication requires guiding the reader top-down: "Communicating the thinking requires only that you guide the reader down the pyramid" (barbaraminto.com, Concept).

* **The Three Core Rules of the Pyramid**:
  In *The Minto Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing, Thinking and Problem Solving* (1996/2010, p. 13), Minto dictates three immutable rules for structuring ideas:
  1. *Rule 1 (Vertical Summarization)*: "Ideas at any level in the pyramid must always be summaries of the ideas grouped below them." (p. 13). A higher-level point cannot be an empty label or topic heading; it must state the deduction or inference that results from the lower-level cluster.
  2. *Rule 2 (Horizontal Homogeneity)*: "Ideas in each grouping must always be of the same kind of idea." (p. 13). Items grouped under a single heading must share the same logical category and level of abstraction, describable by the same plural noun.
  3. *Rule 3 (Logical Ordering)*: "Ideas in each grouping must always be logically ordered." (p. 13).

* **The Four Valid Forms of Logical Ordering**:
  Minto establishes that ideas cannot be randomly listed. There are only four ways to logically sequence points:
  1. *Deductive Order*: Major premise, minor premise, conclusion (or Problem, Cause, Solution).
  2. *Chronological / Time Order*: Step-by-step progression through time or a multi-phase process.
  3. *Structural / Space Order*: Partitioning a physical or organizational whole into MECE components (e.g., geography, business unit, balance sheet categories).
  4. *Comparative / Ranking Order*: Ordering items by degree of importance, scale, or performance (e.g., largest to smallest, highest impact to lowest impact).

* **The Vertical Question-and-Answer Dialogue**:
  Every assertion in a pyramid creates a specific question in the reader's mind ("Why?", "How?", or "What does it mean?"). The author must resolve this question horizontally on the tier immediately below: "Any point you make must raise a question in the reader's mind, which you must answer horizontally on the line below" (Minto, 1996, p. 17).

* **The SCQA / SCQ Narrative Framework**:
  Minto teaches that every business document or presentation must open with a story-form introduction to establish common ground before delivering the answer:
  1. *Situation (S)*: A context or historical baseline that the reader already knows and fully agrees with: "The introduction should begin by establishing for the reader the time and place of a Situation" (Minto, 1996, p. 34).
  2. *Complication (C)*: An external disruption, barrier, or new development that broke the stability of the Situation and created tension: "In that Situation something will have occurred (known as the Complication) that caused him to raise... the Question" (p. 34).
  3. *Question (Q)*: The core business dilemma triggered by the Complication (e.g., "How should we respond?", "Which market should we enter?").
  4. *Answer (A)*: The governing thought, recommendation, or resolution sitting atop the pyramid.

* **Rejection of Intellectually Blank Assertions**:
  Minto warns against summarizing groupings with descriptive labels such as "The company has three objectives" or "There are two organizational problems." Every heading and summary statement must state the consequence, finding, or action implied by the grouping.

### 1.2 McKinsey & Company: Storyline, SCR, Action Titles, Dot-Dash, and Ghost Decks
McKinsey formalized Minto's principles into its daily operational doctrine for client engagements, slide presentations, and MGI research reports.

* **Situation-Complication-Resolution (SCR)**:
  McKinsey standardizes SCQA into the SCR framework. The Situation provides the uncontroversial industry or corporate reality; the Complication identifies the operational threat, profit deterioration, or growth roadblock; and the Resolution presents the recommended strategic transformation.

* **Action Titles and the Read-Through Test**:
  McKinsey rejects descriptive topic labels on slides and section headers (e.g., "Regional Sales Analysis" or "Cost Structure"). Instead, every slide carries an **Action Title** (also called a lead title): a complete declarative sentence that states the core finding, takeaway, or required action.
  - *Standard*: A reader should be able to flip through an entire 50-slide deck, read only the action titles at the top of each page, and understand the complete, logically airtight business case without reading a single bullet or table in the body.
  - *Length*: Typically 1 to 2 lines (under 15 to 20 words), starting with an active verb or clear subject-predicate assertion.

* **Horizontal Logic vs. Vertical Logic**:
  - *Horizontal Logic*: The narrative flow across pages. When all action titles are read in sequence, they form an unbroken, compelling storyline following the SCR or deductive structure.
  - *Vertical Logic*: The internal proof on each individual page. The charts, exhibits, and data points inside the slide body must directly and exclusively substantiate the specific claim made in that slide's action title. Anything that does not support the action title is moved to the appendix or deleted.

* **The Dot-Dash Storyline Outline**:
  Before opening presentation software, McKinsey teams draft the entire presentation narrative in text form using a "dot-dash" outline:
  - *Dots (Bullet Points)*: Represent the governing assertions and action titles for each slide (the horizontal storyline).
  - *Dashes (Indented Sub-bullets)*: Detail the specific data points, analyses, or exhibit sketches required to prove each dot (the vertical logic).
  - This discipline ensures that structural debates happen in text format where restructuring is effortless, preventing wasted effort on discarded slides.

* **Ghost Decks**:
  A ghost deck (or skeleton deck) is a draft presentation where every slide contains its final action title and wireframe placeholder boxes indicating the expected charts, exhibits, or tables, but no actual data, graphic design, or colors. Ghost decks allow engagement managers and client leadership to stress-test the argument and agree on the analytical requirements before junior consultants spend weeks gathering data.

### 1.3 Gene Zelazny: "Say It With Charts" (McKinsey Chart Selection Doctrine)
Gene Zelazny served as McKinsey & Company's Director of Visual Communications for over four decades, training generations of consultants and codifying the firm's data visualization rules in *Say It With Charts: The Executive's Guide to Visual Communication* (McGraw-Hill, 4th ed., 2001).

* **The Three-Step Visual Translation Process**:
  1. *Determine the Message*: What is the specific finding or conclusion? (Formulate the action title).
  2. *Identify the Comparison*: What type of quantitative relationship proves this message?
  3. *Select the Matching Chart Form*: Pick the chart layout that natively displays that comparison.

* **The Five Core Types of Quantitative Comparison**:
  Zelazny proved that any business chart represents one of exactly five quantitative relationships:
  1. *Component Comparison*: Demonstrates the size of each part as a percentage of a single whole.
     - *Primary Chart*: Pie chart (limited to 5 or fewer slices, largest slice starting at 12 o'clock, progressing clockwise).
     - *Alternative for Multiple Wholes*: 100% stacked column or 100% stacked bar chart.
  2. *Item Comparison*: Ranks discrete, non-temporal entities side by side to show relative standing.
     - *Primary Chart*: Horizontal bar chart, sorted in descending or ascending order.
     - *Visual Rule*: Bar spacing must be narrower than the bar thickness.
  3. *Time-Series Comparison*: Shows how a metric changes across continuous chronological periods.
     - *Primary Chart for <= 7-8 Time Intervals*: Vertical column chart.
     - *Primary Chart for Many Time Intervals (> 8)*: Line chart.
     - *Visual Rule*: Time must always run horizontally from left to right.
  4. *Frequency Distribution Comparison*: Shows how many items fall into successive numerical ranges or intervals.
     - *Primary Chart*: Column histogram or frequency polygon (stepped line).
  5. *Correlation Comparison*: Examines whether two variables move together or demonstrate a causal link.
     - *Primary Chart*: Scatter plot (X-Y scatter) or paired horizontal bar chart.

* **Visual Hierarchy and Chart Cleanliness Rules**:
  - *Weight Hierarchy*: Data curves/lines must be thicker than reference axes, and axes must be thicker than background gridlines.
  - *Direct Labeling*: Use direct data labels on bars and lines rather than forcing readers to cross-reference a separate legend or lookup grid.
  - *No Redundant Dual Axes*: Zelazny prohibits showing both direct data values at the end of bars and a numeric scale axis at the margin; use one or the other.
  - *Highlighting*: Apply a strong contrasting color or shade only to the single data series, bar, or slice that embodies the takeaway stated in the action title.

### 1.4 Bain & Company: "Answer First" and Executive Publishing Architecture
Bain & Company champions an aggressive "Answer First" communication philosophy across both client work and public industry reports (e.g., *Global Private Equity Report*).

* **The "Answer First" Philosophy**:
  Bain consultants are trained to state the ultimate conclusion and recommended course of action in the opening 30 seconds of an executive briefing or at the top of page 1. Business executives do not want an academic journey that builds tension until a surprise revelation at the end. Leading with the answer establishes the decision boundary immediately and allows senior stakeholders to interrogate the underlying assumptions where they feel most uncertain.

* **The "At a Glance" Executive Box**:
  In its published macro reports, Bain places an "At a Glance" callout box on the first page of every major chapter. This box contains 3 to 4 bold, bulleted sentences that provide a complete synopsis of the chapter's findings, data shifts, and strategic implications.

* **Figure Action Titles and In-Text Integration**:
  Every chart in a Bain report is labeled as a numbered Figure (e.g., "Figure 1:", "Figure 2:"), immediately followed by a full-sentence action title detailing the empirical trend. Body paragraphs explicitly reference each figure using inline pointers such as "(see Figure 1)" or "*(see Figure 2)*".

### 1.5 Boston Consulting Group (BCG): Report Architecture and Strategic Framing
Boston Consulting Group integrates strategic frameworks (originating with Bruce Henderson's Growth-Share Matrix) with rigorous macro analyses (e.g., *Global Wealth Report*, *Global Asset Management Report*).

* **Detailed Table of Contents**:
  BCG publishes an expansive Table of Contents that lists not only chapter numbers and titles but also full sub-bullet titles for every topic and subsection, enabling executive scanning before reading.

* **Preface and Context Setting**:
  BCG reports regularly begin with a dedicated "Preface" (often featuring a drop cap and multi-decade timeline exhibits) written from the vantage point of senior practice leaders, establishing the historical baseline before analyzing current volatility.

* **Two-Level Exhibit Titling**:
  BCG charts feature a two-tier title: a formal category label followed by a claim title (e.g., "Exhibit 3 - Growth Markets Are Catching Up to the Mature Markets").

### 1.6 McKinsey Global Institute (MGI): Research Publishing Standards
The McKinsey Global Institute (MGI), established in 1990 as McKinsey's economics and business research arm, follows a structured publishing template seen in reports such as *Performance through people* (February 2023).

* **Editorial and Funding Independence Statement**:
  MGI mandates a prominent disclaimer on inside front covers stating: "None of our work is commissioned or paid for by any business, government, or other institution; we share our results publicly free of charge; and we are entirely funded by the partners of McKinsey."

* **"In brief" Executive Synthesis**:
  MGI reports do not label their opening summary as an "Executive Summary." Instead, they use the distinctive title **"In brief"** (conventionally placed on page iv with Roman numeral pagination), pairing a multi-column text narrative with a full-page infographic highlighting the central conceptual framework.

* **Exhibit Discipline**:
  Every exhibit is sequentially numbered ("Exhibit 1", "Exhibit 2"), headlined by an action-oriented research question or finding, categorized with functional headers, and anchored at the bottom with a standardized source attribution line: "Source: [External databases]; McKinsey Global Institute analysis."

## 3. Writing Rules and Conventions

### 3.1 Title and Heading Conventions
1. **The Action Title Rule**:
   - Every slide headline and primary section header must be written as a complete declarative sentence expressing an insight, finding, or recommended decision.
   - *Prohibited*: Topic labels or lookup headings (e.g., "Market Growth", "Cost Reduction Initiatives", "Survey Results").
   - *Mandatory*: Insight assertions (e.g., "Labor-productivity growth lags manufacturing and the total economy", "Spiking interest rates derailed dealmaking in 2023", "P+P Winners achieve 30 percent higher revenue growth per dollar invested").
   - *Grammar*: Subject + Active Verb + Quantified Object/Impact.
   - *Length Constraint*: Keep action titles to 1-2 lines (under 15-20 words). If more space is required, place the extra context in a secondary subhead or the body text.

2. **The "Read-Through" Test (Horizontal Logic)**:
   - A deck or executive report passes the horizontal logic test if an executive can understand the entire narrative, context, problem, core findings, and strategic recommendations solely by reading the action titles in sequence.

3. **Section Trackers vs. Action Titles**:
   - Slide decks use small, unobtrusive section trackers (category labels like "Context", "Market Dynamics", "Recommendations") placed above or alongside the action title to help readers navigate without diluting the action title itself.

---

### 3.2 Page Composition and Structural Rules
1. **The "One Idea Per Page" Rule**:
   - Every slide or report section must convey exactly one governing thought, embodied in its action title.
   - If a slide contains two distinct findings or requires two unrelated charts to explain, it must be split into two separate slides.
   - Every element in the slide body (exhibits, callouts, data tables) must serve solely to prove that single governing thought (Vertical Logic).

2. **"So What?" Callouts and Lead-In Boxes**:
   - Consulting documents do not leave interpretation to the reader. Whenever an exhibit or dataset is presented, a dedicated callout element ("Takeaway", "Implication", or shaded "So What?" box) provides the business consequence.
   - In Bain reports, this is formalized as the "At a Glance" box. In McKinsey reports, it appears as bolded lead sentences or callout sidebars.

3. **Lead With the Core Insight (BLUF / Answer First)**:
   - Within individual paragraphs, place the conclusion in the very first sentence (Bottom Line Up Front). The subsequent 2-4 sentences provide the empirical proof, numbers, and boundary conditions.
   - Executives who scan only the first line of each paragraph must grasp the full intellectual substance of the page.

---

### 3.3 Exhibit, Chart, and Visual Hierarchy Conventions
1. **Zelazny's Chart Selection Matrix**:
   - *Share of whole (percentages)*: Use pie chart (1 whole, <= 5 slices) or 100% stacked column/bar chart.
   - *Rankings (who is biggest/smallest)*: Use horizontal bar chart, sorted descending.
   - *Trends over time*: Use vertical column chart (<= 7 time intervals) or line chart (> 7 intervals).
   - *Distributions (spread of values)*: Use column histogram or stepped line.
   - *Relationships / Tradeoffs*: Use scatter plot or paired bar chart.

2. **Visual Contrast and Highlighting**:
   - De-emphasize neutral baseline data using muted tones (light grey or soft navy).
   - Highlight the focal finding (e.g., the client's position, the winning archetype, the target segment) using a primary brand accent color.
   - Remove visual clutter: Eliminate heavy black gridlines, 3D shapes, drop shadows, and ornamental borders.

3. **Exhibit Metadata Discipline**:
   - *Exhibit Number*: Formal sequential identifier (e.g., "Exhibit 4" or "Figure 2").
   - *Lead Sentence Caption*: State the takeaway directly above the chart.
   - *Notes Block*: Immediately beneath the graphic, define sample sizes (n = ...), currency years, base exchange rates, and geographical coverage.
   - *Source Line*: Always bottom-left or bottom-right, explicitly citing both the raw vendor and the consulting firm's proprietary analysis: `Source: Bloomberg; McKinsey Global Institute analysis` or `Sources: Preqin; Bain analysis`.

---

### 3.4 Paragraph, Sentence, and Tone Conventions
1. **Tone and Voice**:
   - Use objective, authoritative, third-person executive voice.
   - Eliminate academic passive phrasing. Prefer direct active verbs ("Companies achieve higher returns", not "Higher returns were achieved by companies").
   - Ban colloquialisms, marketing jargon, and unsubstantiated superlatives (avoid "revolutionary", "game-changing", "unbelievable").

2. **Sentence Economy and Structure**:
   - Average sentence length should remain between 15 and 22 words.
   - Use parallel grammatical structure in all bulleted lists (e.g., all bullets start with an imperative verb or all start with a noun phrase).

3. **Number and Evidence Conventions**:
   - *Always Quantify*: Never write "Costs rose significantly." Write "Costs rose 18 percent year over year, outpacing revenue growth of 9 percent."
   - *Date and Context Anchoring*: Every metric must be anchored to a specific time period (e.g., "over the 2012-2022 period", "as of third-quarter 2023").
   - *Disciplined Rounding*: In executive text, round numbers to avoid false precision (e.g., "$1.2 trillion" rather than "$1,214,832,100"; "roughly 30 percent" rather than "29.74 percent"), while preserving exact figures in data tables.
   - *Spell Out Percent*: In formal report body text, spell out the word "percent" (e.g., "15 percent"), while using the symbol "%" in chart labels, axis headers, and tables.
